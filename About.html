About the Enterprise

Eight Centuries of Light

How a small band of Poor Clare nuns in Ijebu-Ode turned prayer into candles, and wheat into bread — for the Church and for the world.

Where We Began

A Monastery That Works With Its Hands

The story of St. Clare Candle and Altar Bread Enterprise does not begin with a business plan. It begins, as most things in monastic life do, with prayer — and with the quiet, patient conviction that work offered to God is itself a form of worship. Long before there was a workshop, there was a chapel. Long before there was a product catalogue, there was a community of women who rose at dawn to sing the Psalms, and then asked themselves a simple question: what can we make with our hands that will serve the Church we love?

That question was answered in stages. It began as a small kitchen operation — a handful of Sisters baking altar bread on stone slabs for the priests of the Ijebu-Ode diocese. It grew into a modest candle workshop, first in a converted storage room, then in a dedicated production space. Today, that small seed has become St. Clare Candle and Altar Bread Enterprise — a registered small-scale apostolate operating under the umbrella of St. Clare Monastery Productions, dedicated to the twin crafts of liturgical candle-making and altar-bread baking.

We are located on Plot 1, Monastery Close, with a postal address at P.O. Box 32, Ijebu-Ode, Ogun State, Nigeria. Our gates are humble. Our workshop is not large. But the work that leaves this place — every candle, every host — carries with it the prayer of the women who made it, and the conviction that even the smallest offerings matter when they are offered in love.

From the very beginning, our purpose was twofold. First, to meet the pastoral needs of the Church in Nigeria — to supply priests, parishes, and religious communities with the liturgical goods they need to celebrate the Sacraments with reverence. Second, to create dignified, meaningful work for women and young people from the surrounding community. This second goal is not an afterthought. In a region where employment opportunities can be scarce, especially for women, a monastery workshop is more than a workshop. It is a source of stability. It is a place where hands learn a craft, where wages sustain families, and where the discipline of holy work is passed from one generation to the next.

Our Patroness

St. Clare of Assisi (1194–1253)

Chiara Offreduccio — Clare, in English — was born in Assisi, Italy, in 1194 to a noble and prosperous family. Her mother, Ortolana, was a woman of deep faith; her father, Favorino, was a knight and a man of considerable standing in the city. By the standards of her time, Clare had everything a young woman could want: wealth, beauty, education, and the promise of a comfortable marriage. She gave it all away.

When Clare was eighteen years old, she heard the preaching of a young man named Francesco — Francis of Assisi — who had renounced his family's fortune to live in radical poverty and to rebuild the Church through simple, joyful obedience to Christ. His words lit a fire in her. On the night of Palm Sunday in 1212, Clare slipped out of her family home and made her way to the Porziuncola, the small chapel where Francis and his brothers lived. There, before the altar, she cut off her hair — a gesture of total renunciation — and exchanged her fine dress for a simple habit. Francis received her consecration and placed her temporarily in a nearby Benedictine convent.

Clare's family was furious. Her father and brothers tried to drag her home by force. According to the earliest accounts of her life, Clare clung to the altar of the convent church and, with her own hands, uncovered her head to show them her shorn hair. She told them she had chosen Christ for her spouse and that no earthly power could take her from Him. Her family, shaken by her resolve, withdrew.

Soon, Clare was joined by her younger sister, Agnes, and later by other women from Assisi and the surrounding towns. Francis gave them a small, ruined church — San Damiano — and there they established a community dedicated to contemplative prayer, radical poverty, and manual labor. They called themselves the Poor Ladies. The world would come to know them as the Poor Clares.

Clare lived at San Damiano for more than forty years, until her death in 1253. She served as abbess of her community for most of that time, though she insisted on the title "mother" rather than "abbess." She wore no shoes, slept on the ground, ate no meat, and owned nothing. Her only possessions, in the end, were the rule she had written for her Sisters and the quiet trust she placed in the providence of God. When she died, the Church immediately recognized her as a saint. She was canonized just two years later, in 1255, by Pope Alexander IV.

Perhaps the most famous story about Clare concerns an event that took place during one of the Saracen raids that threatened Assisi in the thirteenth century. A band of mercenaries had broken into the outer cloister of San Damiano. The Sisters were terrified. Clare, though gravely ill, asked to be carried to the wall that faced the attackers. There she had a small ciborium — a vessel containing the Blessed Sacrament — placed in her hands. She raised it high, and according to the account that has come down to us, the soldiers were struck by a sudden fear and fled, unable to look upon the Sacrament. The sisters were spared.

This story, more than any other, explains why the daughters of St. Clare would one day take up the craft of candle-making and altar-bread baking. Clare raised the Blessed Sacrament — and today her Sisters in Ijebu-Ode raise candles to adorn the altars where that Sacrament is confected, and press wheat into the very bread that will be transformed into the Body of Christ.

The Poor Clares

A Tradition of Contemplation and Work

The Order of Poor Ladies — the Poor Clares — has continued for more than eight hundred years without interruption. Today there are thousands of Poor Clare nuns scattered across every continent, living in small, self-contained monasteries, following the Rule that St. Clare herself wrote. It is the shortest and most demanding rule in the Church: a call to total poverty, complete obedience, and unceasing prayer, lived out in the enclosure of a monastery.

The Poor Clare charism is defined by four essential commitments. First, the commitment to poverty. Second, the commitment to contemplative prayer — the Sisters spend hours each day in silent adoration of the Blessed Sacrament. Third, the commitment to community life — the Sisters live not as individuals but as a family. Fourth, the commitment to manual labor — not as a mere necessity but as a spiritual discipline.

It is the fourth commitment — manual labor — that most directly animates St. Clare Candle and Altar Bread Enterprise. Our workshop is not a distraction from contemplative life. It is an expression of it.

The daily rhythm of a Poor Clare monastery is demanding. The Sisters rise in the small hours of the night for Matins and Lauds. They return to the chapel at dawn for Prime, then to the refectory for a simple breakfast. The mid-morning and afternoon hours are devoted to work. Vespers and Compline close the day. Silence is the atmosphere in which all of this unfolds.

The Craft · Part One

How We Make Our Candles

Candle-making is an ancient craft, but it is also an exacting one. A candle that burns poorly is not merely a defective product — it is a disruption of prayer. A candle that smokes, drips, or gutters draws attention to itself and away from the altar. A candle that is made well, by contrast, disappears into its purpose.

Our candle-making process begins with the selection of wax. We use clean-burning paraffin blends, and for special liturgical candles, the traditional beeswax blend — typically 51% beeswax — that the Church has favoured for centuries.

The wicks are cut from high-grade cotton, unbleached and untreated with the heavy metals or chemical stiffeners used by many commercial manufacturers. We size the wick to the diameter of the candle — a critical calculation.

The wax is melted in temperature-controlled pots. Once the wax has reached the right temperature, it is poured slowly into the moulds — one by one — with the wicks already suspended in place. The candles are then left to cool slowly, often overnight.

When the candles have fully cured, they are removed from their moulds, inspected for defects, and finished. The tops are trimmed. The wicks are checked and, if necessary, replaced. Each candle is wiped clean, wrapped in paper, and boxed for shipping.

In all of this, the Sisters work by a simple rule: make it as if you were making it for the altar itself. Because, in fact, you often are.

The Craft · Part Two

How We Make Our Altar Bread

Altar bread is a very different craft from candle-making. It is quieter, smaller in scale, and more demanding of attention. The validity of the Sacrament depends on the bread being made from the right ingredients.

Our altar bread is baked from two simple ingredients: pure wheat flour and water. No oil, no salt, no sugar, no yeast, no preservatives. This is not merely our preference — it is the requirement of the Church for valid matter.

The flour is weighed precisely. The water is measured to the millilitre. The two are combined and worked into a stiff dough. This stage requires judgment that can only be learned by experience.

The dough is then rolled into thin sheets of uniform thickness, then cut into circles using metal cutters of standard size — small for the faithful's communion, larger for the priest's host.

The cut hosts are placed on baking plates and transferred to a low-temperature oven. Baking is a gentle process; the goal is not to brown the bread but to drive off just enough moisture to preserve it.

When the hosts have cooled, they are inspected one by one. Any host with a crack, an irregular edge, or a visible defect is set aside. The remaining hosts are sealed in airtight containers.

The care that goes into this work is difficult to describe. It is not merely professional diligence — it is something closer to devotion. When a Sister places a tray of hosts in the oven, she knows that one day, in some parish in Nigeria, one of those hosts will be lifted up at the words of consecration and become the Body of Christ.

Our Impact

More Than a Workshop

When visitors come to St. Clare Monastery Productions, they often expect to find a small, quiet operation. What they find instead is a place where dozens of lives are quietly touched.

The most visible impact of the enterprise is employment. We create dignified work for local women and youth from the Ijebu-Ode area and surrounding communities. These are jobs that require patience, attention to detail, and a willingness to learn.

For many of the women who work with us, this is their first formal employment. It is the first time they have earned a steady income of their own, the first time they have been able to contribute meaningfully to their household budgets, the first time they have been able to save.

The impact extends beyond employment. Every naira earned by the enterprise supports the charitable works of the monastery: the care of the sick, the support of the poor, the education of children in need, and the formation of new Sisters.

There is also a spiritual impact. The presence of a Poor Clare monastery in Ijebu-Ode is a gift to the wider Church in Nigeria. The Sisters pray daily for the diocese, for its priests and religious, for the sick and the suffering, for the intentions of everyone who asks for their prayers.
