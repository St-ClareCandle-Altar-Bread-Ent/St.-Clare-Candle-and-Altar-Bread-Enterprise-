The Poor Clare Life

A Life Given to Prayer

Behind the enclosure, in silence and adoration, a small band of women offers itself to God — for the Church, for the world, and for every soul in need.

What Is a Vocation?

A Call, Not a Career

A vocation is not a career choice. It is not a decision made in a single moment of enthusiasm. It is a quiet and persistent call — the voice of Christ speaking to a particular soul and inviting her to follow Him along a specific path. For most Christians, that path is marriage, family life, the single life, or the ordained priesthood. For some, it is the consecrated religious life — a life set apart, wholly offered, wholly given.

The religious vocation is one of the great gifts of the Church. It is not a rejection of the world but a gift offered for the world. When a woman enters the monastery, she does not leave behind the concerns of humanity — she takes them into her heart and lifts them to God. Her days are not empty; they are filled with the most fruitful work a human being can perform. She has chosen to spend herself entirely in prayer and penance for the sake of those who will never know her name.

This is why the Church has always held the contemplative life in the highest honour. It is a hidden life — but a hidden life of immense worth. Beneath the silence of a monastery, the world is quietly sustained.

The Poor Clares

A Contemplative Community

The Poor Clares are a contemplative community. This means their first calling is not to teach, to nurse, or to run schools — but to pray. They live within the enclosure of the monastery, in silence, in community, and in radical poverty. They own nothing of their own. Their hours are not marked by appointments but by the bells of the Divine Office, which call them to prayer throughout the day and night.

Their enclosure is not a prison. It is a garden. It is a place where the distractions of the world are set aside so that the soul can attend to the one thing necessary — the presence of God. From within this enclosure, the Poor Clares reach out to the entire world through prayer. They intercede for priests and religious, for the sick and the dying, for sinners and for saints, for the living and for the dead.

Every Poor Clare monastery in the world is a small candle burning before the Blessed Sacrament. It does not seek attention. It does not demand recognition. It simply burns — faithfully, quietly, constantly — for the glory of God and the salvation of souls.

The Work of Prayer

Their Apostolate Is Intercession

Every religious community has an apostolate — a form of work through which it serves the Church. For the Poor Clares, that apostolate is prayer itself. Their "work" is not measured in products or projects but in hours spent before the Blessed Sacrament, in decades of the Rosary, in the slow and patient recitation of the Psalms.

This does not mean their lives are idle. It means their labour is hidden — carried out in a place where no one sees, in a manner that no metrics can measure. And yet the Church has always taught that this hidden labour is the very foundation of her mission. When a missionary preaches in a distant land, he is supported by the intercession of those who will never leave their cloister. When a parish celebrates the Holy Sacrifice of the Mass, the prayers of contemplative communities rise with it. When a soul in despair turns to God, the hidden prayer of the Poor Clares is often part of the answer.

The Sisters intercede for the whole world. They remember the intentions of everyone who asks for their prayers — the sick, the suffering, the dying, the lost, the Church, and the nation. And they offer their own daily labour — the making of candles, the baking of altar bread — as a further act of prayer, offered to God through the work of their hands.

The Hours of Prayer

A Day Marked by the Divine Office

The Sisters' day is anchored in the Liturgy of the Hours — the official prayer of the Church. Eight times each day, from before dawn until well after nightfall, the bell rings and the community gathers in the chapel. There, in unison, they recite or chant the Psalms, listen to the Word of God, and lift up the needs of the Church and the world.

🌙

Office of Readings & Lauds

In the deep silence of the early morning, the Sisters rise for the Office of Readings and Morning Prayer — the first great offering of the day to God.

🌅

Terce & Sext

As the working day unfolds, brief pauses for prayer keep the soul recollected — the Psalms of the Little Hours accompany the labour of the hands.

🔔

None & Vespers

The afternoon and evening hours gather the community again before the Lord, at the summit and close of the day's work.

✨

Compline & Night Silence

The day ends with Compline, the final prayer before the Great Silence. Then the monastery rests — awaiting the bell that will call it back to prayer once more.

Between these hours, the Sisters keep the spirit of prayer alive in everything they do — the melting of wax, the baking of bread, the washing of dishes, the writing of letters. For them, prayer is not a department of life; it is the air they breathe.

Hidden, but Never Lost

What Their Prayer Achieves

It is a mystery of our faith that God chooses to act through the prayers of His people. He does not need us — and yet He has willed that His grace flow through us, that the world be sustained in part by the intercession of those who love Him. The Poor Clares take this truth seriously. They do not pray in order to be seen. They pray because prayer changes things.

The Sisters pray for the Church — for the Holy Father, for bishops and priests, for missionaries and catechists, for religious and for seminarians. They pray for the sick and the suffering, for those who have no one to pray for them, for the dying and for the dead. They pray for peace in troubled lands, for the conversion of sinners, and for the perseverance of the just.

And they pray for Nigeria — for her families, her children, her parishes, and her people. In a country that carries many burdens, the silent intercession of the Poor Clares is one of its quietest and strongest blessings.

A Hidden Witness

Why the Contemplative Life Matters

To the eyes of the world, the life of a Poor Clare can seem strange — even wasteful. Why would a young woman give up marriage, career, travel, and independence to live behind a wall, in silence and poverty? The answer is simple: because she has heard the voice of Christ, and she has said yes.

That "yes" is not a rejection of the world. It is an offering for the world. It is a witness — a public sign that God is real, that prayer matters, that eternity is more important than time. Every Poor Clare monastery is a small but powerful proclamation of this truth. It says, silently but unmistakably, that there is more to human life than what we can see and touch.

This is why the Church speaks of the contemplative life as the "hidden apostolate." It is hidden because it takes place in silence and enclosure. It is an apostolate because it bears fruit in the lives of countless people who will never know its origin.

For Those Who Feel Called

Discerning a Vocation

If you have found yourself drawn — quietly and persistently — to a life of prayer, do not dismiss the feeling. A vocation is often first noticed as a small, faint tug on the heart. It grows with prayer, with spiritual direction, and with conversation. It does not always announce itself with certainty; often, it simply refuses to go away.

Women who feel called to the Poor Clare life should speak with a spiritual director, with their parish priest, or with a Poor Clare community directly. The Sisters of St. Clare in Ijebu-Ode are happy to receive enquiries from those discerning a vocation — and to pray for all who are seeking to know the will of God in their lives.

You are not alone in this. Every Poor Clare who has ever entered a monastery began where you are now — with a question, a hope, and a prayer.

A Mutual Blessing

Partner With Us in Prayer

Your intention will be remembered in the daily prayers of the community — in the Liturgy of the Hours, in the Rosary, and in silent adoration before the Blessed Sacrament.

You do not need to be a priest or religious to benefit from the intercession of the Poor Clares. The monastery is a spiritual refuge for the entire Church. Questions About the Vocation

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a Poor Clare?

A Poor Clare is a woman consecrated to God in the contemplative religious life, following the Rule written by St. Clare of Assisi. She lives in community, in enclosure, in radical poverty, and in unceasing prayer.

What do contemplatives do all day?

They pray. Their day is built around the Liturgy of the Hours, silent adoration, the Rosary, and manual labour offered to God. Their life is simple, structured, and profoundly peaceful.

Can visitors enter the monastery?

The enclosure is reserved for the Sisters, but visitors are welcome in the chapel for Mass and are invited to contact us in advance to arrange a visit.

How do I know if I have a vocation?

Vocation is discerned through prayer, spiritual direction, and conversation. If you feel drawn to the contemplative life, contact us or speak with your parish priest. We will pray for you and help you discern.

Do the Sisters have jobs outside the monastery?

No. The Poor Clare life is enclosed — the Sisters do not leave the monastery for work. Their apostolate is prayer, and their manual labour (candles, altar bread) takes place within the monastery.

Can I ask for prayers without being Catholic?

Yes. The Sisters pray for all people of goodwill. If you have a need, you are welcome to share it with us — and we will carry it to God.
